\subsection{Large Language Models and Prompt Engineering} 
Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} introduced the \textit{Transformer} architecture, which marked a pivotal milestone in deep learning and paved the way for the era of large language models (LLMs). \fref{fig:transformers} illustrates the architecture of the Transformer, which employs an encoder-decoder framework, where the encoder transforms an input sequence of symbolic representations $(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ into a corresponding sequence of continuous representations $(z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_n)$, upon which the decoder autoregressively produces an output symbol sequence $(y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_m)$. The core mechanism of the Transformer is \textit{Self-Attention}, which allows the model to compute attention scores for each word in a sentence or document in parallel, modeling the influence of each word on every other word. 

\begin{figure*}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter2/transformers.png}
  \caption{The Transformers - model architecture~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.}
  \label{fig:transformers}
\end{figure*}

Given an input sequence of vectors $(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$, where $x_i$ represents the embedding of the $i$-th token. Each $x_i$ will be linearly projected into query ($q$), key ($k$), and value ($v$) vectors by three trained weight matrices. In general, the attention function is computed efficiently by packing the queries, keys, and values into matrices $Q$, $K$, and $V$. The resulting output matrix is defined by the scaled dot-product attention mechanism:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:attention}
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}
where $d_k$ denotes the dimension of the key vectors.

In practice, the Transformer adopts \textit{Multi-Head Attention} mechanism rather than a single attention function. The input is splited into multiple heads that perform attention functions in parallel, the results of which are concatenated and linearly combined to produce the final output. This design enables the model to simultaneously capture diverse relationships and patterns, allowing it to better understand the context and capture long-range dependencies.

Notably, the parallelizable nature of the self-attention allows it to outperform recurrent neural networks (RNNs) in terms of training efficiency, making it possible for the efficient pre-training of large-scale models on GPUs. Once trained, these pre-trained language models (PLMs) can achieve remarkable performance by fine-tuning on specific downstream tasks with relatively small amounts of task-specific data.

Based on the architecture of Transformer, PLMs have branched into three main categories: encoder-only (e.g., BERT), decoder-only (e.g., GPT), and encoder-decoder (e.g., T5). Among them, the decoder-only architecture has emerged as the dominant choice in recent years due to its effectiveness in natural language generation tasks and superior generalization capabilities, and representative models such as GPT, Claude, Qwen, and DeepSeek have all leveraged this architecture. 

Guided by scaling laws~\cite{kaplan2020scaling}, model performance improves consistently as model parameters, computational budgets, and data size increase. This growth facilitates the emergence of advanced capabilities, such as zero-shot and few-shot learning, which typically absent in smaller models below a certain scale. Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020language} observed that few-shot performance improves more rapidly as model size increases compared to zero-shot performance, highlighting that larger models possess stronger in-context learning capabilities.

To effectively harness the emergent capabilities of LLMs, \textit{Prompt Engineering} has become a critical discipline to guide the model's output distribution toward desired outcomes. Much of its efficacy is rooted in \textit{In-context learning (ICL)}~\cite{schulhoff2024prompt}, which allows the model to learn from examples or instructions embedded in the prompt without updating weights. 

Many prompting techniques have been proposed to enhance the performance of LLMs~\cite{sahoo2024systematic}: (1) \textit{Few-shot prompting} provides the model with a limited number of input-output examples as context, helping it induce an understanding of the given task, recognize task-relevant patterns and respond in appropriate formats. (2) \textit{Zero-shot Prompting}, which relies solely on task descriptions or instructions without any examples, requiring the model to generalize from its pre-trained knowledge without any examples. Common strategies, such as \textit{Role-playing} (e.g., \textit{You are a senior software engineer.}) and \textit{Emotion Prompting} (e.g., \textit{It is really important to my career.}), are also widely used. (3) \textit{Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting}, which encourages the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps, improving performance on complicated tasks that require multi-step reasoning. Related techniques such as \textit{Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT)} and \textit{Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT)} extend this idea by structuring the reasoning process, enabling systemic exploration of multiple reasoning paths. Together, all of these techniques have been shown to mitigate hallucinations and improve the accuracy of complex tasks, and have substantially broadened the applicability of LLMs, enabling them to tackle increasingly complex tasks more reliably.


\subsection{Automated Workflows and Autonomous Agents}
LLMs' comprehension and reasoning skills makes them compelling candidates for the central ``brain'' or general-purpose controllers within intelligent agentic systems. Based on their architectural design, these systems can be broadly categorized into two types~\cite{effetive_agents}: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Workflows} coordinate LLMs and tools through a sequence of predefined, explicit steps. This design offers predictability and consistency, making it suitable for well-defined tasks.
    \item \textbf{Agents} allows the LLM to determine how to accomplish tasks by organizing the processes and tool usage on their own. This design provides flexibility to revise strategies in response to real-time feedback and adapt to complex and dynamic environments.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure*}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter2/agent.pdf}
  \caption{Augmented LLM-based agentic system~\cite{effetive_agents}.}
  \label{fig:agent}
\end{figure*}

As depicted in \fref{fig:agent}, the core block of an agentic system is an augmented LLM equipped with capabilities such as retrieval, tools, and memory. Retrieval (e.g., \textit{Retrieval Augmented Generation}~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}) helps the LLM access external knowledge bases to reduce hallucinations. Tool Use (e.g., \textit{Function Calling}~\cite{function_calling_guide} and \textit{Model Context Protocol}~\cite{model_context_protocol}) acts as the ``hand'' of the agentic system, enabling the LLM to perform actions and interact with the real world. Memory (e.g., short-term context and long-term storage~\cite{agent_guide}) allows the LLM to persist, recall and reason over historical information.

This agentic system can perceive changes from the environment and execute actions to influence it in return. Within this loop, the LLM serves as the central intelligent engine that understands the state of the environment, queries necessary information, selects appropriate tools, and determines what information to retain in memory at each step. By leveraging frameworks like \textit{ReAct}~\cite{yao2022react} to integrates reasoning and acting or \textit{Reflexion}~\cite{shinn2023reflexion} to enable self-reflection with dynamic memory, LLMs gain stronger reasoning and planning abilities, enabling more autonomous decision-making.






\section{Formal Methods in Software Analysis}

\subsection{Symbolic Execution and SMT Solver}
Symbolic execution is a formal program analysis technique powered by constraint solving~\cite{baldoni2018survey}. It explores multiple execution paths of a program by treating program inputs as symbolic representations rather than concrete values. Formally, symbolic execution maintains a pair of \textit{symbolic state} and \textit{path conditions} $<\sigma, \pi>$, where $\sigma: \text{Var} \to \text{Expr}$ is the mapping from variables to symbolic representations, and $\pi$ is a conjunction of constraints that must hold for execution to follow the current path. At the beginning, $\sigma$ is initialized as an empty mapp and $\pi$ is set to $\text{true}$. Every time a conditional branch (e.g., an if-statement \textit{if($e$)}) is encountered, the engine forks: one adds $\sigma(e)$ to $\pi$ (true branch) and the other adds $\sigma(\lnot e)$ to $\pi$ (false branch). An execution path is considered feasible if and only if its accumulated path condition $\pi$ is satisfiable. When a path terminates or reaches a target assertion, a constraint solver is invoked to determine the satisfiability. If $\pi$ is satisfiable, the solver provides a concrete set of input assignments that guarantees the program will follow that exact execution path.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{minipage}{0.8\textwidth} 
        \begin{minted}[frame=single]{c}
void foo(int a, int b) {
    int x = a + b;
    if (x > 11) {
        if (a > 3) {
            assert(false);  // bug
        }
    }
}
        \end{minted}
    \end{minipage}
    \caption{A simple C program}
    \label{fig:c}
\end{figure}

Consider the simple C program in \fref{fig:c}. Symbolic execution begins with symbolic inputs $\alpha$ and $\beta$ for $a$ and $b$ respectively, and maps $x$ to $\alpha + \beta$. Upon reaching the first if-statement, it forks into two paths: the true branch records $\pi_1 = (\alpha + \beta > 11)$ and the false branch records $\pi_2 = \lnot(\alpha + \beta > 11)$. Continuing along $\pi_1$, the execution forks again at the second if-statement. The true sub-path accumulates $\pi_{11} = (\alpha + \beta > 11) \land (\alpha > 3)$, which is then submitted to the constraint solver upon reaching the assertion. Since $\pi_{11}$ is satisfiable (e.g., $\alpha = 4$ and $\beta = 8$), symbolic execution reports a feasible bug-triggering path along with a concrete counterexample. Similarly, the remaining paths $\pi_2$ and $\pi_{12}$ are checked and found to be safe. 

While symbolic execution is theoretically robust, its practical application is often hindered by challenges such as \textit{path explosion}, modeling of complex data structures, and environmental interaction problems. To address these limitations, \textit{concolic execution} was introduced as a hybrid approach that utilizes concrete execution to drive symbolic execution. Rather than forking at every branch, concolic execution never forks. It executes the program with concrete inputs while simultaneously tracking symbolic constraints. After a concrete run is completed, the engine identifies a branch condition along the path, negates it, and asks the SMT solver for a new set of concrete inputs that satisfy this modified constraint. Tools such as DART~\cite{godefroid2005dart}, CUTE~\cite{sen2005cute}, and KLEE~\cite{cadar2008klee} have demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach across large real-world codebases.

The efficiency of symbolic execution heavily depends on the underlying constraint solver, which is typically an SMT (Satisfiability Modulo Theories) solver 
such as Z3~\cite{de2008z3} and CVC5~\cite{barbosa2022cvc5}. SMT solving is the process of determining the satisfiability of first-order formulas. It generalizes the Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) problem, which concerns the satisfiability of propositional logic formulas, by introducing background theories such as linear arithmetic, bit-vectors, and arrays. During symbolic execution, each path constraint is represented as a conjunctive formula, and submitted to the SMT solver as a satisfiability query. If it is satisfiable (SAT), the solver returns a model that assigns concrete values to the symbolic variables; if it is unsatisfiable (UNSAT), it indicates that the execution path is unreachable and can be pruned safely. 

\subsection{Program Facts and Datalog}
Program facts are a structured representation of information extracted from source code for the purpose of automated reasoning and analysis. They encode observable properties of a program, such as the existence of entities (e.g., functions, classes, variables), their attributes (e.g., names, locations, modifiers), and relations between them (e.g., containment, calls, inheritance), in a form suitable for systematic querying and inference.

Program facts are derived mechanically from source code through language-specific frontends, typically by parsing the code and traversing intermediate representations such as abstract syntax trees or control-flow graphs. Each extracted fact captures a single, well-defined aspect of the program, and together they provide a precise, machine-readable abstraction of the program’s structure and behavior. Importantly, program facts are \emph{descriptive}: they record what is present in the program, rather than how analyses should be performed.


\subsubsection{Datalog}

Datalog is a declarative logic programming language rooted in first-order logic and database theory. A Datalog specification consists of a set of rules that describe how new facts can be derived from existing ones.

\begin{definition}[Datalog Rule]
A Datalog rule has the form:
\begin{equation}
    R_0(t_1, \dots, t_k) \leftarrow R_1(u_1^{(1)}, \dots, u_{m_1}^{(1)}), \dots, R_n(u_1^{(n)}, \dots, u_{m_n}^{(n)})
\end{equation}
where each $R_i$ is a predicate symbol. The atom on the left-hand side is called the \emph{head}, and the atoms on the right-hand side form the \emph{body}.
Each argument $t_j$ or $u_j^{(i)}$ is either a constant or a variable.
\end{definition}

The rule is interpreted as follows: for any assignment of variables to constants that makes all body atoms simultaneously true, the corresponding instantiated head atom is also true. Variables thus serve as placeholders that allow a rule to match and relate multiple facts.

\begin{definition}[Datalog Program]
A Datalog program is a finite set of Datalog rules evaluated over a given set of ground facts. Its semantics is defined as the least fixpoint of rule application: rules are repeatedly applied to derive new ground facts until no further facts can be inferred.
\end{definition}

Datalog supports recursion and operates under a monotonic, set-based semantics, making it well suited for expressing transitive and structural properties such as reachability, dependency propagation, and hierarchical relations in program analysis.


\subsubsection{Program Facts in Datalog}

In program analysis, program facts are represented in Datalog as \emph{ground predicate instances}. Each predicate schema corresponds to a specific kind of program entity or relation, while each extracted program fact instantiates that schema with concrete constants derived from the source code.

For example, a predicate describing function definitions may be declared as:
\begin{minted}{prolog}
.decl function_definition(file_path: symbol, function_name: symbol, start_line: number, end_line: number, param_count: number, is_async: symbol, containing_class: symbol)
\end{minted}

An extracted function definition in the source code gives rise to a ground fact of this predicate, with all arguments bound to concrete values such as file paths, names, and line numbers.

Datalog rules operate over these ground program facts using variables to range over matching predicate instances. The body of a rule specifies patterns over existing program facts, while the head defines a new fact to be derived whenever the body is satisfied under some variable assignment. Through repeated rule application, Datalog derives higher-level program properties—such as reachability, dependency relations, or structural patterns—from the underlying set of extracted program facts.

This representation cleanly separates \emph{fact extraction}, which records concrete observations about the program, from \emph{logical inference}, which declaratively specifies how additional properties are derived.

\section{Neuro-Symbolic AI}

The pursuit of artificial intelligence has been a relentless human endeavor, historically divided into two main categories: \textit{Symbolic AI} and \textit{Sub-symbolic AI}. Symbolic AI represents knowledge through formal, declarative symbols based on rules and logic, and leverages these symbolic representations to reason about and solve complex problems. While this paradigm offers strong interpretability and verifiability, it struggles with scalability and flexibility when confronted with the complexity of the real world, as exhaustively encoding all relevant domain knowledge into hand-crafted rules proves intractable in practice. 

Subsequently, sub-symbolic AI emerged, placing artificial neural networks at its core. Rather than explicit rules, this paradigm relies on algorithms to extract statistical patterns from data, identify latent relationships, and make predictions through learned representations. Exemplified by machine learning, deep neural networks, and generative models, sub-symbolic AI has achieved remarkable success in areas such as pattern recognition and natural language processing. Although large-scale models have been observed to exhibit reasoning-like behaviors brought by emergent ability, they still lack explicit logical structure, making rigorous reasoning and formal verification fundamentally difficult.


Neuro-symbolic AI draws inspiration from the System 1 and System 2 theory~\cite{kahneman2011thinking}. System 1 is fast, intuitive, and parallel, akin to the capabilities of deep learning, while System 2 is slow, deliberate, and sequential, resembling symbolic reasoning. Neuro-symbolic AI seeks to integrate these two approaches to create systems that leverage the strengths of both and ultimately produce a superior hybrid AI model possessing reasoning abilities. In the context of software engineering, as illustrated in \fref{fig:neurosymbolic_approaches}, there are two main neurosymbolic approaches~\cite{chen2025comparative} to improve logical reasoning: 
\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item \textbf{Integrative approach.} Symbolic reasoning is embedded within the neural network.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid approach.} Performing symbolic reasoning with a separate symbolic solver, distinct from the neural network.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure*}[ht]
  \centering

  \caption{Integrative and hybrid neurosymbolic approaches~\cite{chen2025comparative}.}
  \label{fig:neurosymbolic_approaches}
\end{figure*}

The hybrid approach demonstrates greater potential for advancing general logical reasoning, as it preserves the capabilities and advantages of existing
LLMs while providing a more interpretable reasoning chain.
